# README spec

`README.md` at the repository's root is written from this spec. To change the README, change this
file and regenerate it ("regenerate README.md from docs/README_SPEC.md"), so the two never drift
apart. Hand edits to `README.md` are lost at the next regeneration unless they are carried back here.
Regenerating edits the existing `README.md`: change only what the spec's change calls for, and keep
everything else word for word. Review it with `git diff README.md`.

The pictures come from `tools/readme_shots.py` (one `bw_ui --screenshot` command line per picture),
into `docs/readme/NAME.png`, 1280 px wide:

```sh
cmake --build --preset release && python3 -I tools/readme_shots.py          # all of them
python3 -I tools/readme_shots.py bench traces                               # some
```

View every picture after taking it. The slow Machines (BW-2001 at about 0.33×, BW-80 at 0.2×) need
their `--after` raised on a slower computer.

## Audience

Someone landing on the GitHub page: a retro-computing or electronics hobbyist, or a player who
likes puzzle games. Not a contributor: `AGENTS.md`, `docs/ARCHITECTURE.md` and the recipes serve
those, and the README doesn't link to them.
Also, while we welcome issues and request, we will probably not accept contributions, as it is hard for me to understand what a contribution to an ai-coded project is and how to avoid drift.

## Voice

Casual, like the author showing a friend the thing they made. Short sentences. "You" is the player.

Avoid what reads as machine-written:

- no "dive into", "delve", "journey", "unleash", "seamless", "robust", "powerful", "immersive",
  "elevate", "whether you're X or Y", "it's not just X, it's Y", "look no further";
- no em dashes: commas, colons, parentheses or two sentences;
- no lists of three adjectives, no rhetorical questions as openers, no exclamation marks in a row;
- no emoji, no bold scattered through sentences (bold only in the tools list's names);
- no "In this README…", no closing pep talk ("Happy hacking!", "Enjoy!");
- headings are plain nouns or short phrases, sentence case.

Plain facts beat adjectives: "117 Puzzles on 14 Machines" over "tons of content".

## Sections, in order

### 1. Title

`# Bodgewire`, then one line: a game about fixing old digital computers, chip by chip.

### 2. The AI notice

First paragraph after the title, before anything else: the code was generated by Claude (Anthropic's AI), directed by a human. People who'd rather avoid AI-made projects can stop here, no hard feelings. One short paragraph, said plainly, without apology or defensiveness.

### 3. The web demo version.

Then the live web version (P8b), one short paragraph: try it in the browser at https://fstark.github.io/bodgewire/, every Machine, slower than the desktop build (a fast computer helps), no pop-out windows. Not playable, but a good idea on what it looks like.

### 4. The goal

Simulating the repair of old digital computers: late-70s, early-80s boards built from TTL chips, then real 8-bit computers. Every signal is simulated, so a fault is a physical thing (a cracked trace, a dead chip output, a solder bridge) and the symptoms come out of the circuit by themselves; nothing is scripted. Two or three sentences. The game design's motto, "Every fault has a cause", may appear once.

### 5. What it is

The player's view, a little salesy but true: you are at a repair bench, a broken Machine in front
of you, a post-it saying what's wrong ("The LED never lights."). You have the Board, the manuals
(User Manual, Service Manual, datasheets) and real instruments. Find the fault, fix it, power it
back on. 117 Puzzles on 14 Machines, from a five-chip LED blinker to copies of the Commodore PET
2001 and the Sinclair ZX80. Count Puzzles and Machines from `machines/*/puzzles/*.bwp` and
`machines/*/` at each regeneration.

Then the launcher picture (`launcher`): each Machine with its paragraph, and a post-it per Puzzle.
One sentence: a repaired Puzzle's post-it keeps its tick on the launcher, from one launch to the
next. One sentence on the last post-it, "Random fault": a fault picked at random each time it is
opened, checked to show before the bench opens; no count for those. Then (the user, 2026-11-25)
that they never run out: one can be anywhere on the Board, and the Thank-you Card offers
another; and the binder's Give up page, which tells what it was.

### 6. A repair, step by step

The Counter's first Puzzle, "Dark LED" (`machines/010-counter/puzzles/01-dark-led.bwp`), told as a
short walk-through, one picture per step:

1. `bench`: the bench as it opens: the Board, the post-it, the shelf of tools at the bottom.
   Five 74LS161 counters divide a 1 MHz crystal down to about one blink a second; the LED stays
   dark.
2. `traces`: hover a chip and its traces light up, with every pin named. U5's pin 11 (QD) is the
   one that drives the LED.
3. `continuity`: power off, the multimeter's black lead clipped on the LED's pad, its red lead
   held on U5 pin 11: its LCD shows dashes, no beep. The chip and the LED are not connected, though a
   trace runs between them.
4. `crack`: zoom in on that trace: a hairline crack across the copper, about half way.
5. `mended`: the soldering iron bridges the crack with a blob of solder; power on, the LED
   blinks.
6. `repaired`: three seconds later the owner's card says thank you, with the next Puzzle one
   click away; the post-it gets a tick. The game checks the repair itself: the Board must build
   the same circuit as the fault-free one.

Say the crack can be repaired with the soldering iron. Don't spell out a general strategy
beyond this one case.

### 7. Tools and instruments

The shelf's items, each with one line from its tooltip (`src/ui/shelf.cpp`, `kItems`), in four
`###` subsections, each with its pictures:

- **Tools**: grease pencil, chip puller, soldering iron, cutters, bodge wire. Then `pencil`: the
  Dark LED's Board with checks on U1–U4 and a cross on the Trace to the LED, the Puzzle's
  reasoning half done.
- **Instruments**: logic probe, logic analyzer, oscilloscope (4 channels), multimeter (volts,
  continuity). Then, each with a sentence saying what it shows:
  - `analyzer`: the LED computer's 6502 starting, SYNC, PHI2 and R/W above the address and data
    buses read as values: the reset vector fetched at FFFC/FFFD (00, F8), then F800's first
    instructions. (The single signals list above the buses: the clips are applied before the
    groups.)
  - `scope`: the Test Pattern Generator's VIDEO (triggered on it, so inside a visible line: a
    pulse per vertical line of the grid) and SYNC_N below.
- **The binder**: User and Service Manuals, datasheets, a memory chip's contents, one picture
  each, all but the datasheet on the BW-2001 with BASIC's banner on the TV; one sentence on the
  aA key on its cover (the text larger, in three steps, kept for next time):
  - `binder-manual`: the Service Manual's Components, every reference a link to the Board;
  - `binder-datasheet`: the Counter's U5, a 74LS161: pinout with each pin's Net, its function;
  - `binder-code`: UH7 (F800–FFFF) disassembled as 6502 code (not UH4: F000 is BASIC's message
    text and disassembles into nonsense);
  - `binder-glyphs`: the character ROM UA2, glyph by glyph.
- **And the rest**, no pictures: the post-it, the Machine's own devices (TV, keyboard, paddle
  knobs) in windows that pop out to their own desktop windows, the controls (power, pause / slow
  motion, a quarter of real time / real time / as fast as possible, Reset: undo every repair, the
  pencil marks stay), the view buttons beside the Board (P8f, `src/ui/view_buttons.cpp`: fit the
  whole Board, real or plain look, pick the copper under the chips, hold a button or key down,
  flip the Board).

Then one line on the kinds of faults met (from the `fault` lines of the Puzzles): cracked traces,
stuck chip outputs, solder bridges, bad joints, bad memory cells, dead switches. No fault names
that would spoil a given Puzzle.

### 9. Building it

From a clean machine to the game running, as commands to copy:

- what is needed: git, CMake ≥ 3.28, Ninja and clang, the only compiler supported on every
  platform (the user, 2026-11-23; `docs/STYLE.md`): Apple's on macOS, clang 23 on Linux; no
  version list for other compilers; on macOS the Xcode command line tools plus
  `brew install cmake ninja`; on Linux, below;
- a very powerful machine, said plainly before the Mac commands, with the numbers: on the
  development Mac (an Apple M5 Max) the BW-2001 runs at about 0.33× and the BW-80 at 0.2× (the speed
  ratio `bw_ui --screenshot` prints; take it again at each regeneration), the small boards at
  1.00×;
- SDL3, Dear ImGui and Catch2 are fetched by CMake on the first configure (network needed once);
  the ROMs are in the repository;
- `git clone https://github.com/fstark/bodgewire.git`, `cmake --preset release`,
  `cmake --build --preset release`, then `build/release/src/ui/bw_ui`;
- optional: `ctest --preset release -j` (a few minutes);
- a `### Linux` subsection after the Mac commands (P8a, 2026-11-22), tried on Ubuntu 24.04 (X11)
  on an Intel i7-6700K, kept short (the user, 2026-11-23):
  - clang 23 assumed installed, not explained (it comes from apt.llvm.org);
  - one short `sudo apt install` line with the main dependencies only: `git cmake ninja-build`,
    `libstdc++-14-dev` (clang takes `<print>` from it: `clang-23` itself pulls GCC 13's library,
    which has none) and SDL3's main X11, Wayland and sound packages (`libx11-dev libxext-dev
    libxrandr-dev libxcursor-dev libxi-dev libxkbcommon-dev libwayland-dev libdecor-0-dev
    libasound2-dev libpulse-dev`);
  - the first configure names the compiler, `CC=clang-23 CXX=clang++-23 cmake --preset release`
    (CMake remembers it), then the same build and run lines as on the Mac;
  - the speeds there, said to be a very slow machine (the user, 2026-11-22), from the same
    `bw_ui --screenshot` ratio and README shots, built with clang 23: on the i7-6700K the small
    boards at 1.00×, Paddle Tennis 0.76×, the BW-2001 0.12× and the BW-80 0.08× (take them again
    when Linux is tried on another machine);
  - X11 by default, through XWayland on a Wayland desktop (2026-11-27: on Wayland, ImGui has no
    desktop windows, so a double-click filled the bench instead of popping out); one sentence:
    `SDL_VIDEO_DRIVER=wayland` for native Wayland, every object then staying inside the main
    window as on the web;
  - the tests: an older KiCad than 8 (Ubuntu's own is 7) fails the Board checks (`drc_*`); with
    no KiCad they are left out;
- honest platform note: developed on macOS; Linux builds and runs (above); Windows untried.

## Keeping it current

- A new Machine: a shot in `tools/readme_shots.py`, a row in §8's table, the counts in §5.
- A new tool or instrument: §7, from the shelf's tooltip, and a picture if it has a window.
- A change to the Dark LED Puzzle or the Counter's Board: §6's shots (the crack's coordinates in
  `tools/readme_shots.py` come from the Board: U5 pin 11 and D1 pad 1 in
  `machines/010-counter/board/counter.kicad_pcb`).
- The screenshot flags the shots use: `--probe`, `--meter A,B` (two pins: the black lead clipped
  on A, the red one held on B, power off), `--zoom X:Y:W`, `--repair`, `--press`, `--knob`, `--type`, `--focus`, `--mark`,
  `--clip`, `--group`, `--sample`, `--view`, `--hook`, `--timebase`, `--manual`, `--contents`,
  `--text-size` (`bw_ui` usage line).
